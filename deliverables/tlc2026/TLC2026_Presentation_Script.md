## Slide 2 - One institution can be experienced very differently

**On slide**

For one student, a problem is resolved quickly.

For another, the same institution can feel impossible to navigate.

The difference is not always ability, effort, or entitlement.

Sometimes it is knowing which rule applies, what evidence matters, and when to
act.

**Speaker notes**

My starting point is personal but the question is institutional. When I arrived
at UCT, an accommodation crisis was resolved within hours after I spoke to an
O-week leader. At the same time, I met students who experienced the same
institution very differently. I began asking why institutional support could be
so close for some people and effectively invisible to others.

This is a reflective starting point. It is not offered as evidence about UCT as
a whole.

## Slide 3 - Students navigate an institution as well as a curriculum

**On slide**

Students are expected to learn.

They are also expected to navigate:

Progression | Registration | DP/DPR | Concessions | Appeals | Prerequisites
| Curriculum change

When this navigation becomes opaque, administrative complexity can displace
educational conversation.

**Speaker notes**

University success involves more than passing assessments. Students must also
work out when a process applies, who owns it, what the deadline is, which
evidence is relevant, and whether a decision is final or provisional.

The point is not that standards should disappear. The question is whether the
knowledge needed to meet them is available early enough and clearly enough.

## Slide 4 - Publication is not the same as intelligibility

**On slide**

Policy can be public and still be difficult to use.

Students need to know:

What applies to me?  
Why does it apply?  
What information was used?  
What is provisional?  
What can I do next?

**Speaker notes**

The problem is not simply that rules are hidden. Rules may be distributed across
handbooks, faculty rules, programme requirements, department practice and
individual context. A student often does not need another PDF; they need help
following the chain of reasoning without being given a false answer.

## Slide 5 - The research question

**On slide**

How can institutions make their reasoning intelligible to the people they
govern without diminishing institutional authority?

Personalising policy without privatising it.

**Speaker notes**

This phrase is important. Personalising policy does not mean inventing a
private rule for each student. It means explaining how the same approved policy
applies differently when legitimate institutional context is different.

An explanation should preserve common rules, evidence, uncertainty, and the
right to human review.

## Slide 6 - The Curriculum Reasoning Engine is the first working proof

**On slide**

Reviewed policy  +  accepted facts

Deterministic evaluation

Traceable explanation

Rule version | Evidence lineage | Plain-language reasoning | Review route

**Speaker notes**

The project began as a Curriculum Reasoning Engine. It represents a reviewed
policy as structured rules, evaluates accepted facts against a specific policy
release, and produces a trace that can be explained in plain language.

The key claim is modest: the prototype demonstrates that this form of governed,
deterministic explanation is technically possible. It has not been tested as an
operative UCT decision system.

## Slide 7 - The architecture has boundaries because the stakes are real

**On slide**

AI may assist with transcription, structuring and explanation.

AI does not decide.

Raw documents do not decide.

Incomplete records remain visible as incomplete.

Authorised people remain responsible for policy, facts, exceptions and review.

**Speaker notes**

This is not a system that tries to make uncertainty disappear. When information
is incomplete or provisional, it should say so. When an exception requires a
committee or advisor, the system should show the route rather than simulate the
authority.

That is both a technical and ethical position: institutional knowledge already
exists. The task is to represent it responsibly, not generate a new authority.

## Slide 8 - Curriculum rules revealed a larger institutional problem

**On slide**

Senate and university rules

Faculty handbook

Department and programme requirements

Student record and authorised context

Current institutional position

**Speaker notes**

While building curriculum logic, I kept discovering that a departmental rule
could not be understood in isolation. It inherited meaning from faculty and
university rules, and sometimes from an authorised concession, appeal or
curriculum-year decision.

That is why the project became an Institutional Reasoning Engine. Curriculum is
the first domain, not the entire thesis.

- Reflective account of prototype development; no claim that this hierarchy is

## Slide 9 - Legibility should strengthen responsibility, not remove it

**On slide**

Students need a fair chance to act early.

Institutions need students to communicate, attend, prepare and use processes
responsibly.

The goal is capable institutional participation.

**Speaker notes**

Scoping conversations complicated my initial thinking. It would be too easy to
say that all difficulty is institutional failure. Students have responsibilities
as well, especially in courses connected to professional formation.

The project should therefore not help students evade standards. It should help
them understand the standards, their position, and the appropriate route to
seek help or challenge an error.

## Slide 11 - The next stage is research, not rollout

**On slide**

1. Study how students and staff encounter institutional opacity

2. Co-design explanations, routes and safeguards

3. Test a limited, ethics-approved pilot

4. Evaluate understanding, agency and the quality of human support

**Speaker notes**

The next honest step is not to deploy a large system. It is to conduct an
ethically approved study, work with real institutional knowledge, and test a
limited use case carefully. The meaningful evaluation questions concern student
understanding and the quality of the human conversations that follow, not only
technical accuracy.

## Slide 12 - The question I hope we leave with

**On slide**

The aim is not to make students rely less on universities.

It is to help them understand how institutional reasoning applies to their own
journeys.

How might we make institutional knowledge easier to act on before it becomes a
crisis?

**Speaker notes**

I do not want institutions to become less institutional. I want their rules,
reasons, uncertainty and human routes to be more intelligible to the people
asked to live within them.
